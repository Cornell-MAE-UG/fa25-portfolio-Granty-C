Baseline calculations

I modeled the course’s M42 steel wrench as a cantilever with an end load. The lever arm L is 16.0 in, thickness b is 0.500 in, bending width h is 0.450 in, and elastic modulus E is 32.0 × 10⁶ psi.

End forceF = T/L = 600/16.0 = 37.50 lbf
Second moment of areaI = bh³/12 = 0.500(0.450)³/12 = 0.00379688 in⁴
Outer-fiber bending stressσ = T(h/2)/I= 600(0.225)/0.00379688 = 35,556 psi = 35.56 ksi
Load-point deflectionδ = FL³/(3EI)= 37.50(16.0)³ / [3(32.0 × 10⁶)(0.00379688)] = 0.4214 in

Why stress differs. In Ansys I fixed the upper 0.400 in of the drive and loaded the end face. Stress concentrates at the fixed edge, giving 113.40/35.56 = 3.19 times the nominal beam stress.

Why deflection differs. The head and exposed drive can rotate in the solid model. That adds 0.4688 − 0.4214 = 0.0474 in of travel (11.2%) compared with the hand model’s perfectly fixed root.

Final CAD and material

Full-size CAD drawings (PDF) · View the 3D assembly

Custom 465 stainless steel · H950

I chose H950 for its fracture toughness of 61.4 ksi√in. At the same stress and crack size, that gives 61.4/15 = 4.09 times the fracture margin of M42. Changing the steel alone brought the original grip travel to only 0.470 in, so I also changed the section.

Material inputs. E = 28.7 Mpsi, ν = 0.28, yield = 220 ksi, and ultimate = 240 ksi. The fatigue strength is 124 ksi at 100,000 fully reversed cycles (R = −1).

Sizing the flexure by hand

For a rectangular beam at fixed torque, σ = 6T/(bh²) and δ = 4TL²/(Ebh³). Narrowing the beam increases travel faster than stress. When I narrowed the beam to 0.300 in, I calculated nearly 3 in of grip travel at full torque. That was more travel than I needed, and the fracture safety factor had dropped to 1.93. I widened the beam to 0.360 in to get back above the required 2.00 while keeping at least an inch of travel.

For the final profile I used the local section inertia I(x) and bending moment M(x) from the distributed grip load. Integrating curvature to the grip center at x = 22 in gives the deflection.

Peak nominal bending stressσmax = max [M(x)h(x)/(2I(x))]= 52.66 ksi at x = 2.055 in
Grip-center deflectionδ(22) = ∫022 (22 − x) M(x)/[E I(x)] dx= 1.450 in

At that bending stress, the hand safety factors are 4.18 for yield, 2.94 for fracture, and 2.35 for fatigue.

I applied F = T/L = 600/22.00 = 27.27 lbf laterally over the 1.520 in² grip face, giving a uniform traction of 17.94 psi. In the fixed-drive model, the upper 0.400 in of the drive cannot move. In the socket model, each flat can push against the socket or lift away as the torque reverses.

Final safety factors and travel

I used the larger peak from the two torque directions for yield and fracture. For fatigue, I took half the difference between the two stress tensors at each node. The largest normal stress amplitude was 65.55 ksi.

The socket model meets all four course targets. With the drive faces fixed, the peak stress rises as the mesh is refined. That model gives 1.32 for yield and 0.89 for fracture. An extra fatigue check for the peened drive including mean stress gives 1.48 against 1.50, so I would test the drive in a real socket before rating it.

Predicted scale sensitivity. The pointer travels 1.1362 in across the scale from zero to +600 in·lbf. Its average sensitivity is Δs/ΔT = 1.1362/600 = 0.001894 in per in·lbf, about 0.095 in per 50 in·lbf tick. The tip also moves 0.130 in toward the head, so the graduations follow a 5.040 in radius arc.

Weight, cost, and manufacture

2.080 lb assembled · $7.92 USD in finished metal. I multiplied each CAD volume by its density. For the H950 body, 6.9944 in³ × 0.2829 lb/in³ = 1.979 lb. At $3.855/lb the body costs $7.63. The 304 tube adds $0.20 and the aluminum parts add $0.09. Buying the starting steel blank alone costs about $34.77 before machining, heat treatment, and calibration.

Machinability. Aged H950 cuts slowly (Granta reference speed 22 surface ft/min). I’d rough-machine the annealed blank, age it, and then finish and peen the drive.

Recycling and corrosion. The H950 body and 304 tube are recyclable once separated from the aluminum indicator parts. H950 resists fresh water well. Granta lists seawater service as restricted.

Reflection

What would matter in a tool I sold?

I’d check whether an oily glove slips and whether the pointer is readable at an angle. A replaceable pointer would let someone repair a dropped wrench.

What would I need to learn?

I’d need to quantify calibration uncertainty, measure how hand position affects the reading, and price heat treatment and inspection for a small batch.

Who could teach me?

I’d review the drawing with a machinist and heat treater, then work with a calibration technician to apply known torques in both directions and compare sockets.

How would I keep learning?

I’ve used beam wrenches and always liked that the only movement comes from the material flexing. Seeing how much the socket changed my stress prediction reminded me why I need to test my assumptions. On future builds I’d record a prediction and check it against a measurement.
