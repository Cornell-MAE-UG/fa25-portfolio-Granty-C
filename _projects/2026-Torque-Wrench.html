Beam torque wrench

I’ve used this type of wrench before. What fascinates me is that the steel itself does the measuring. Pull on the grip and the beam bends just enough to move a scale past a pointer.

The baseline design

Start with a plain bar.

The original M42 steel beam is 16.00 in from the drive to the force, 0.450 in wide in the bending plane and 0.500 in thick. At rated torque, the end load is 600/16 = 37.50 lbf.

Where the stress went

The hand model puts its highest tensile stress at the outer fiber of the beam root. In the solid, the maximum principal tension occurs at the edge of the fixed drive band. It is 3.19 times the nominal beam stress. Peak axial tension occurs at the sharp drive shoulder and is 2.08 times the hand value. Refining the mesh raises the fixed-edge peak from 113 to 141 ksi while the grip motion changes by just 0.16%.

Why the end moved farther

The solid predicts 0.0474 in more travel, about 11.2%. I fixed the upper 0.400 in of the square drive and spread the force over the end face. The free part of the drive and the head can rotate, and a small rotation moves a point 16 inches away. The hand model assumes a perfectly fixed beam root.

With E = 32.0 Mpsi and ν = 0.29, the beam has Iz = 0.003797 in⁴. I used the course’s 370 ksi tensile strength for the brittle M42 screen, KIC = 15 ksi√in and fully reversed fatigue strength of 150 ksi. The hand factors are 10.41 for strength, 1.06 for fracture and 4.22 for fatigue. The required travel is 1.00 in and the required fracture factor is 2.00.

For the fracture check I used KI = 1.12σ√(πa) with edge-crack depth a = 0.040 in. The 16-in rectangular beam weighs 1.044 lb and costs $6.26 at the course rate of $6/lb. I reconstructed the solid from the course drawings, including the 0.500-in drive projection and 0.225 in of material behind its centerline. The Ansys result uses 5,113 quadratic tetrahedra and 9,654 nodes.

My design

Let the beam flex.

I moved the grip center to 22.00 in and thinned the working width to 0.360 in. The wider head carries torque into the square. The slim middle does the bending, and the full-width grip extends past the load mark so there is room for a hand.

How I chose the section

Width matters twice here. Reducing it raises bending stress roughly as 1/h², but it raises deflection as 1/h³. At a fixed rated torque, a longer lever increases the travel without increasing the nominal root moment. The 0.300-in trial bent nearly three inches, but its hand-calculated fracture factor was only 1.93 and its fatigue factor was 1.55. That’s why I went back up to 0.360 in.

How I treated the transitions

The top and bottom perimeter edges have a 0.060-in radius. The head and grip flow into the beam through tangent transitions. The drive has rounded corners and a 0.100-in circular fillet at the square-to-head shoulder. The pointer seat and scale pad attach away from the high-strain beam, avoiding drilled holes or engraved stress raisers in the flexure.

All trials use 0.500 in thickness. For the final hand check I integrated M(x)/[EI(x)] along the plan profile with the section corner radii. A load evenly spread from x = 20 to 24 in gave 1.450 in mean grip travel and 1.139 in at the scale. The highest nominal beam stress was 52.66 ksi at x = 2.055 in. The hand factors there were 4.18 for yield, 2.94 for fracture and 2.35 for fatigue.

On comparable meshes, enlarging the circular drive fillet from 0.080 to 0.100 in reduced peak socket-model tension from 76.8 to 67.5 ksi. An elliptical shoulder gave 70.0 ksi and higher equivalent stress.

Why I curved the scale

At full torque the tip moves about 1.136 in across the plate and 0.130 in toward the head, measured in the plate’s own frame. A 5.040-in-radius track follows that sweep. The 25 predicted tick positions are within 0.0002 in of the arc. I put each 50 in·lbf tick at its own projected position so the tip stays beside the line all the way to either end. Testing with known torques would tell me the actual accuracy.

Material choice

I selected wrought Custom 465 stainless steel, H950 (UNS S46500). The M42 baseline had plenty of nominal strength but little fracture margin. In Granta EduPack’s Level 3 Aerospace database I compared the age-hardened grades and opened the H950 record. It combines strength at the square drive with toughness at the shoulder and excellent freshwater resistance.

Finite element analysis

Where the wrench works hardest.

The beam sets the readout. The drive carries the highest local stress. I meshed the exported STEP solid in Gmsh, refined the mesh around the drive and its shoulder, and imported the ten-node tetrahedra as SOLID187 elements into Ansys Mechanical APDL 2026 R1.

First I clamped the square drive as shown in the assignment. Then I modeled how a socket would actually react on the four flats. Contact can push on a flat and lift off the opposite side, and the loaded side switches when the torque reverses. Both models grip the upper 0.400 in. This comparison matters because a perfectly fixed edge concentrates stress in a way a compliant socket does not reproduce.

Mesh checks, socket assumptions and fatigue method

The production model has 30,958 quadratic tetrahedra and 50,870 structural nodes. In an independent 52,325-element check, maximum socket-model tension changed from 68.81 to 68.85 ksi and alternating equivalent stress from 91.98 to 92.54 ksi. Fixed exterior faces gave 115.0, 135.0 and 173.4 ksi on successive meshes. Fixing every node inside the upper 0.400 in also gave a rising edge stress. The grip and readout displacements converged much sooner than these edge peaks.

I assumed a seated, frictionless socket with compression-only normal springs (COMBIN39), zero initial clearance and smooth contact relief at the ends and edges of each flat. The nominal stiffness was 110 million lbf/in³. Varying it from 60 to 200 million changed the tensile peak from 68.1 to 69.6 ksi on the same mesh, with less than 0.00003 in change in lateral scale travel. The torque reaction balanced 600 in·lbf. The single axial datum at the drive tip carried negligible force.

Results in the tables use full-model nodal averaging of the solid stresses. Ansys’s surface-only PowerGraphics display gives slightly different peaks. At positive and negative rated torque those display values are 71.34 and 70.43 ksi. Even using 71.34 ksi gives a fracture factor of 2.17. The baseline’s sharp fixed edge is much more sensitive to the display method, giving 157.64 ksi in PowerGraphics. Original Ansys plot, +600 in·lbf · Original baseline plot

I formed the alternating stress tensor at each common node by taking half the difference between its positive- and negative-torque tensors. I checked the largest absolute principal value for normal fatigue and the von Mises equivalent of that same tensor for a multiaxial screen. The latter is a screening approximation for the peened material under contact. The fracture calculation checks fast opening-mode fracture at peak tension. A crack-growth life estimate would also need da/dN data, a threshold and a crack-shape history.

Stock and machining

Granta lists H950 at $3.63–$4.08/lb. I used its midpoint for the body and planning rates of $3/lb for 304 and $2.50/lb for 6061. A 1.125 × 1.125 × 25.25 in square blank at the annealed density of 0.2822 lb/in³ weighs about 9.02 lb, or about $34.77 at that same steel rate. The finished-metal estimate uses 0.285 lb/in³ for the tube and 0.0975 lb/in³ for aluminum. Supplier premiums, adhesive, markings, labor, heat treatment, peening and calibration are additional. ATI 304 data

The aged steel cuts slowly. Granta lists a machining speed of 22 surface ft/min under its reference conditions. I’d rough-machine the annealed blank, age to H950, finish the drive and radii, then peen the highly stressed surfaces to the tested finish and check the flats with a gauge. Most of the cost of a one-off would be in those steps.

Corrosion and recovery

Granta rates freshwater durability excellent and flags Custom 465 as recyclable. Its metal-corrosion record restricts seawater service. I’d keep chips segregated, give the scale a replaceable attachment in a production version, and separate the aluminum and stainless parts before recycling. The bonded mounts in this design make that last step harder.

The stock contains much more metal than the wrench. If I were making a batch, I’d compare machining from a bar with a near-net forged blank and measure the actual chip return, tool life and distortion after heat treatment.

Reflection

From a model to a tool I’d trust.

I worked on this project individually. The surprising part was how much the socket model changes the peak stress while the pointer reading barely moves.

What else would matter in use?

I’d try it with oily gloves and with the fastener in a cramped spot. A clear zero mark and a pointer close to the scale both matter when I can only see it at an angle. I’d also check socket retention, overload behavior and whether a bent pointer can be replaced without scrapping the beam.

What would I still need to learn?

I can calculate a scale pitch, but I’d need to learn how to assign it an uncertainty. A cantilever estimate gives 0.032 in of pointer sag in the least favorable orientation, leaving about 0.010 in of clearance to the marks. I’d check for rubbing, measure how hand position changes the reading, and get real quotes for heat treatment and inspection.

Where would I get that knowledge?

I’d take the drawing to a machinist and a heat treater, then spend time with a torque-calibration technician. I’d use the torque-tool calibration standard to plan a deadweight test in both directions and compare several sockets. I’d keep the readings, uncertainty budget and teardown photos alongside the simulation.

How would I keep learning?

I’ve used beam wrenches and always liked that they work without moving parts beyond the material flexing. I hadn’t thought much about how the socket changes the stress until this project. On future builds I want to write down one prediction before testing, keep the result even when it surprises me, and revisit the assumptions that made the difference.
