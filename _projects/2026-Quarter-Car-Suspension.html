Abstract

We compared a coil spring and a sealed air spring in a model of one corner of a car. We modeled the body and wheel masses separately, linearized the air spring at ride height, and compared the two systems with Bode plots and Python simulations. On a 50 mm bump crossed in 0.20 s, air lowered RMS body acceleration from 1.113 to 1.009 m/s² and settled 0.42 s sooner. It also used 1.27 mm more suspension travel and lowered minimum tire load by 0.151 kN. Spring settings and road frequency changed the tradeoff.

My contribution

I focused on Python simulation, comparing the body and tire responses, and making the result interactive. Our group also developed the coil model and the air spring linearization.

MAE 3260 methods

Force balances and state space, Taylor linearization, transfer functions and Bode plots, and time-domain simulation with parameter sweeps.

1 Mechanical model

Week 6F used a single mass to study a suspension spring and damper in parallel [1]. We added a separate wheel mass and tire spring so we could study tire unloading and wheel hop alongside body motion.

Body displacement zs, wheel displacement zu, and road height r are positive upward from static equilibrium. Positive compression q means the wheel has moved upward relative to the body. Gravity and spring preload cancel in the incremental force balances.

q = z u − z s F = k q + c q ˙
(1)
m s z s ¨ = F m u z u ¨ = k t ( r − z u ) − F
(2)

A positive suspension force F acts upward on the body and downward on the wheel. The tire is linear. The two models share the same masses, tire rate, and baseline damping.

2 State space and transfer functions

At steady state, a constant change in road height moves both masses by the same amount. The displacement gains are 1 at zero frequency, and acceleration, compression, and incremental tire load return to zero. The direct term in D gives the tire’s immediate response to a road-height change.

3 Air spring and linearization

The air case uses a sealed chamber and the same damper. Compression reduces volume at a constant effective area. The trapped gas follows a polytropic pressure-volume law [4].

V ( q ) = V 0 − A e q p ( q ) = p 0 ( V 0 V ( q ) ) n
(6)

The gas law uses absolute pressure. The lift uses gauge pressure, so the ride-height pressure balances the body weight. Subtracting this static lift gives the incremental force in the air model.

p 0 = p atm + m s g A e p 0 = 297.525  kPa absolute
F a ( q ) = A e ( p ( q ) − p 0 ) F = F a ( q ) + c q ˙

We used a first-order Taylor expansion at ride height, where the incremental spring force is zero [5]. The tangent slope replaces the coil rate in the linear state matrices and transfer functions.

k air = [ d F a d q ] q = 0 = n p 0 A e 2 V 0 k air = 11.603  kN/m F a ( q ) ≈ k air q
(7)

At the same static pressure, a larger air chamber lowers the tangent rate. The nonlinear force stiffens as the chamber compresses.

4 Frequency response

The eigenvalues of A give two stable modes. The magnitude of each pole’s imaginary part divided by 2π gives the damped frequency. The negative real part divided by the pole magnitude gives the damping ratio. In the wheel-hop mode the wheel moves about 17 times as far as the body.

We evaluated the body transfer function at s = jω and plotted its magnitude and phase [6]. Linearized air moved the body peak from 1.09 to 0.92 Hz and lowered it from 8.85 to 7.45 dB.

Air has more body motion at 0.9 Hz, near its own body resonance, and less at 1.1 Hz, near the coil resonance. Near wheel hop at 9 Hz, the body moves about 1 mm while the tire-load amplitude reaches about 3 kN.

5 Road bump response

Both cars started at rest and crossed a 50 mm raised-cosine bump in 0.20 s. We integrated the four states through 6 s using fourth-order Runge–Kutta with a 0.5 ms step. The air model uses the nonlinear pressure law for this test.

r ( t ) = H 2 ( 1 − cos ( 2 π t T ) ) 0 ≤ t ≤ T  (zero otherwise)
(8)

Peaks use absolute magnitudes. RMS acceleration uses the first 2 s. Settling starts at bump exit and ends when the body stays within ±2% of its own peak displacement from equilibrium. Tire load includes the 3.532 kN static load.

The air spring reduced RMS acceleration by 9.3% and used 1.27 mm more suspension travel. It also reduced the minimum tire load by 0.151 kN. Both cars retained positive tire load for this run.

6 Parameter effects and conclusions

We changed one parameter at a time and kept the same baseline bump. For each air chamber volume, the static lift stayed the same. The rates in Table 6 describe the slope at ride height, and the results use the nonlinear air force.

A softer coil or a larger air chamber reduced RMS acceleration, allowed more travel, and lowered minimum tire load.

More damping shortened settling and reduced peak travel in both models. It also raised RMS acceleration as the damper transmitted more of the wheel’s fast motion to the body.

Matched spring rate and model checks

At 5.439 L the air tangent matches the 16.00 kN/m coil rate and the linear traces coincide. With the nonlinear gas law at that volume, peak body motion is 31.23 mm for air and 28.50 mm for the coil.

We checked the transfer functions against the state-space response. Halving the time step changed each baseline metric by less than 0.1%. The maximum body-displacement error of the air linearization grew from 4.5% of the nonlinear peak on a 25 mm bump to 11.8% on a 65 mm bump. On an 80 mm bump, the predicted tire load dropped below zero and the continuous-contact assumption broke down.

Conclusion

The 7.50 L air setup reduced body acceleration on the baseline bump. The coil kept more load on the tire and used less travel. Road frequency, spring rate, and damping changed that tradeoff. The results cover vertical motion of one corner with a sealed, constant-area air chamber and a linear tire and damper.
